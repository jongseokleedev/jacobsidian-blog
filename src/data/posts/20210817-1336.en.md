I wanted a mentor — someone in my life who would make me think, "I want to live like that person." But from my school days onward, the "person you admire" section of every self-introduction form I filled out remained blank. So I started wandering into local bookshops. At first I read books written by famous people. Since I had no role models nearby, I went looking in books. There is something thrilling about drawing wisdom from those who have succeeded. The catch, of course, is that they never have time for you personally — but reading their books is the next best thing: a conversation on your own terms. Recently I enjoyed Phil Knight's *Shoe Dog*, the story of how Nike was born. One book, and you get to sit across from the founder of Nike. How extraordinary is that?

When I was a student I was stubbornly averse to reading. Apart from required textbooks, I barely opened a book. So concentrating on long texts was hard for me. In the beginning, because my focus faded with longer pieces, I read a lot of short essays. Essays have a particular power: they make you revisit a small moment of daily life you had carelessly passed by. That said, essays are highly hit-or-miss — some land with resonance in every single sentence, while others leave you finishing the last page still unsure what the author was trying to say. I've come to think this isn't about the book being good or bad, but about whether your own circumstances align with what the writer is expressing. An essay that means nothing to you today may read entirely differently when your situation changes. Lately I read Kim Young-ha's *The Reason I Read, Travel, Meet People, and Study* — a travel essay that felt especially poignant during the COVID-19 pandemic when travel had become a distant memory.

As I grew more comfortable with the written word, I started picking up full-length novels. Reading fiction is like taking a brief trip to another world. These days everyone talks about VR and the metaverse, but I think virtual reality has existed for a long time under the name "novel." Fiction *is* a virtual reality experience. If I had to name the novel I've enjoyed most, it would be Haruki Murakami's *Norwegian Wood*. Murakami's characteristic, aching portrayal of loss kept me trapped in his fictional world for a long while — I simply could not find my way out.

Not wanting to finish a book and retain nothing, I started writing book reviews on my blog — steadily, regardless of whether anyone read them. It was the first time in a long while, perhaps ever, that I worked diligently at something purely for my own satisfaction with no tangible outcome in sight. As I kept writing, something unexpected happened: a bookstore reached out, saying they wanted to feature one of my reviews in their "Book of the Day" section. I had just been writing for fun, so the idea that someone had read it and found it worth sharing moved me deeply. I felt embarrassed — I never thought my writing was good enough to appear anywhere — but I trusted the editor and said yes. I even received a small honorarium. A modest amount, but it felt foreign in the best way. I was so used to earning money by gritting my teeth through work; receiving payment for something I simply enjoyed felt strange and meaningful. The money was almost too precious to spend.

Knowing that people were reading what I wrote made me want to try writing about more than just books. Whenever I encountered a moment in daily life I wanted to preserve, I wrote an essay. Somewhere along the way, I began to dream of gathering those pieces into a book of my own. And so here I am — having found books through reading, having found writing through books, and now circling back to make a book of my own. Perhaps this is what people mean when they say: the way forward is found inside books.
