*(Feat. The Death of Figma)*

If you've adopted AI tools but collaboration still doesn't feel any faster, the first thing to question isn't the tools themselves — it's the layer your work runs on.

This piece makes two arguments. **First, that in the AI era, collaboration efficiency is determined not by how sophisticated your tools are, but by which working layer you choose.** And second, **that the thing builders should scrutinize first is precisely what feels most familiar and most obvious — the tools and processes they've always taken for granted.**

## You've Got Figma MCP. Why Doesn't Anything Feel Faster?

In the early days of AI adoption, most teams follow a similar path. They move past code autocomplete and start layering in tools that bridge the gap between design and engineering. Connecting Figma MCP (Model Context Protocol) to convert mockups into code is one of the most common examples.

The results, more often than not, fall short of expectations. Individual steps get faster, but the end-to-end experience of shipping a feature — from first sketch to production — doesn't feel meaningfully different. You extract code from a mockup and someone has to clean it up by hand. The designer updates a design, and how that change flows into the codebase becomes a fresh negotiation every single time. **The more AI tools you add, the more handoff points you create.** The structure works against you.

The question to ask here isn't "which tool should we add next?" We keep refining the bridge — but what if the problem isn't the bridge at all? What if the two systems shouldn't need one in the first place?

## The Day We Converted Frontend Code to HTML, Then Back to Figma

There's a pattern that exposes this structural problem most clearly. Imagine a tight deadline: the engineer builds the screen in code first, without any wireframes. The implementation exists before the Figma canvas does.

Now the designer wants to refine the details. But they want to work in Figma. There are two options: upload a screenshot of the app (it's a bitmap — it can't be decomposed into components), or convert the code into an HTML mockup, parse that HTML, and reconstruct it as components on the Figma canvas.

Even the second path isn't clean.

> **Fun Fact —** Figma doesn't distinguish between `outline` and `border`. Dev Mode outputs all strokes as `border`, so anything that should have been an `outline` — focus rings, for instance — ends up implemented as a `border` instead. The result: a button that's 32px tall in one context and 34px in another. For a designer, it's just a stroke either way. For an engineer, the swap breaks the layout entirely. One of the [issues that has quietly cost developers](https://forum.figma.com/suggest-a-feature-11/differentiate-between-border-vs-outline-35451) — from a tool that bills itself as a design-development collaboration platform.

![Pasted image 20260527181252](/images/Pasted%20image%2020260527181252.png)

These mismatches compound: some components come back as generic boxes that only approximate the original shape. The designer works on top of a slightly-off canvas. The engineer receives the result and re-implements it in code. When the dust settles, both people spent most of their time on work that wasn't really their job. One built a reverse-parsing tool that wasn't even accurate. The other redrew a design on top of a screenshot.

![diagram-reverse-parse-loop](/images/diagram-reverse-parse-loop.svg)

None of this would have happened if both sides had agreed from the start to work on a shared code-based layer. **When you haven't decided where your source of truth lives, both sides end up recreating it from scratch — every time.**

## What Going Figma-Free Actually Looks Like

The direction of the solution is straightforward. Pull the reference point for design work off the Figma canvas, and put Markdown, HTML, and CSS in its place. Move to a rule-based layer that code can consistently read and interpret.

One misconception is worth addressing upfront. **This does not mean designers should write frontend code.** "Codebase" here doesn't refer to deployed JavaScript or component code. It refers to a working layer where design intent and system definitions are expressed in a form that code — and AI — can parse.

In practice, the workflow looks like this. A designer makes a natural-language request: "Summarize this design system as a DESIGN.md" or "Update the Primary Color change in the spec doc." The AI structures that intent into a set of rules. The result renders immediately as HTML/CSS and serves as the mockup. No specific tool is required — any environment that can translate natural-language intent into a code-friendly layer will produce the same result. **What matters isn't the name of the tool. It's the choice of working layer.**

There's another chronic problem with canvas-based workflows that this also solves. When a designer draws mockups in Figma, the hover state, the error state, the empty state, the loading behavior — these almost always arrive in engineering incomplete. Gaps don't come with comments. If there's no comment, the gap becomes whatever the engineer decides. The burden of finishing the design gets silently transferred.

The key is the strictness of the layer itself. **On a code-based layer, mismatches surface immediately. On a canvas, mismatches accumulate silently — invisible until a human eye catches them.** Working with AI requires the former, not the latter.

## What Changes When You Switch the Working Layer

![diagram-workflow-before-after](/images/diagram-workflow-before-after.svg)

The first change you feel after switching to a code-based layer is speed.

In a canvas-based workflow, shipping a single screen runs on a multi-day cycle. You receive the mockup, notice a gap, ask about it, receive an update, translate it into code, then wait for the designer to catch details in QA before making another round of revisions. On a code-based layer, the same work takes hours. The designer's intent, captured in Markdown, is simultaneously the mockup and the spec. Code references it directly. The handoff points where clarity bleeds away are mostly gone.

Small design QA rounds get structurally faster too. A change like "can you add 4px of padding here" becomes editing a single token. What used to require the designer to update Figma and the engineer to manually propagate that value across the codebase now becomes one line — applied globally and consistently. Design details stop getting lost in implementation. The source of truth is in one place, visible to both humans and AI alike.

## The Hard Part — Figma Is Not a Tool. It's a Way of Thinking.

The biggest point of resistance in making this transition is designer adoption. And this is not a technical problem.

The first time a designer faces a terminal, the reaction is almost always the same. Black screen. Unfamiliar commands. Markdown text full of symbols that haven't been rendered yet. "This is a developer's job" comes naturally. Even if you keep an HTML-rendered preview open beside it, the difficulty remains. The cycle of "edit a text-based rule, then check what it looks like" is simply unfamiliar.

This isn't just a learning curve. **For a designer, Figma isn't a tool — it's a way of thinking.** Placing an element on the canvas, moving it around, adding color, pulling it back — that sequence *is* the designer's cognitive process. Asking them to move that process into a text editor isn't a suggestion to change tools. It's closer to asking them to rewire how they think. That weight deserves to be taken seriously from the beginning, and the transition should be approached gradually.

Two approaches are effective at reducing resistance.

- **Let them feel the payoff directly.** When a designer experiences QA feedback being applied in minutes instead of days — and sees their intended details survive the implementation intact — the resistance to the new layer starts to dissolve.
- **Frame it as an industry-wide shift, not a team quirk.** Show that this isn't an idiosyncratic choice your team is making, but a direction the whole industry is moving toward, sooner or later.

Forcing a tool on someone lands differently than showing them where things are going.

## How to Meet a New Paradigm

There are two takeaways worth carrying into your work as a builder.

**First: in the AI era, collaboration is determined by the working layer, not the tools.** Refining how your AI tools integrate and moving your working layer to code are not the same work. Most of the time you spend fine-tuning Figma MCP is time spent polishing the bridge. A more polished bridge doesn't move the river. The leverage is in shifting the working layer itself.

**Second: the most familiar tool is the first one worth questioning.** Figma is a good tool. It has earned the trust of designers and engineers over many years, and there are real reasons it became the industry standard for collaboration. But familiarity obscures first principles. Many of the workflows we treat as "just how it's done" are actually artifacts of past constraints — shapes carved out by an earlier era's limitations. When a paradigm shifts, distinguishing between the two is one of the most important things a builder can do.

## Closing

Distilled to its simplest form, the conclusion of this piece is: *"drop Figma."* But that's not how I want to leave it. Figma is still a good tool, and not every team should reach the same conclusion.

What's worth checking, though, if you're building products: **is your team's work happening within a single layer, or on a bridge between two?** Is the bottleneck you're experiencing the same kind as printing out an email, writing your reply by hand, scanning it back in, and sending it — all because you hadn't questioned why you were doing it that way?

If you've adopted AI tools but collaboration still doesn't feel faster, check the coherence of your working layer before you blame the tools.

If there's one concrete action to take away: before adding another AI integration, try defining your design system in a code-friendly format — a Markdown doc, a design token file, anything your tools can read directly. Whether that small experiment leads to a full working-layer migration or just a useful supplement to your existing workflow will vary by team. But you can't know the answer without running the experiment.
